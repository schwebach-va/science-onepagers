Everybody’s Science

by Dr. J. Reid Schwebach

Everybody’s Science is about how scientific thinking actually works — and who it belongs to. Not facts to trust or refuse, but a way of reasoning with evidence that anyone can run and anyone can check.

For readers who just want to think more clearly. For the reader who’s been told not to trust any of it and has good reasons to wonder — and who wants to know whether what they’re sure of is something they checked, or something they were handed. For teachers who want science taught as a process, not a syllabus. For policymakers, school boards, anyone who has to act on evidence they can’t verify themselves.

And for anyone who loves a skeptic, or is one.

I am a high school biology and chemistry teacher. Before that I grew up on a family farm in New Mexico, taught in New York City public schools, earned a PhD in microbiology and immunology, worked on tuberculosis in a lab, held a faculty appointment, and spent several years in science policy at the National Research Council, AAAS, and the National Science Foundation. The book is written from the classroom, which is the vantage point that made the rest of it make sense.

The structure is the argument

The book does not open by asserting that evidence deserves your trust. That argument fails on exactly the reader it most needs to reach, and there is good research on why: handing better facts to someone whose position is anchored in identity tends to harden it rather than move it. A book that just handed you better facts would be repeating the error it is trying to diagnose.

So it demonstrates first and argues second. Ten chapters of close, checkable observation come before the book turns to the hard part — a streambank in Virginia, a warming ocean, a vaccine race, the chemistry of an ordinary morning. The direct argument — why any of this deserves your trust, how misinformation gets built on purpose, and what to do with a claim you’re not sure of — arrives in Chapters 11 and 12. A good deal of it is memoir, and on purpose: the evidence for what a scientific life actually is has to come from somewhere, and the only one I can show you from the inside is mine.

  1. Part I — Wonder. Where scientific attention comes from: a farm, a streambank, deep time in the rock.
  2. Part II — How Breakthroughs Happen. Mastery you never finish, how scientists choose questions, why progress is rarely a lone genius — and how science argues with itself on purpose.
  3. Part III — The Science That Explains Us. Chemistry, water and climate, immunity and evolution, in the ordinary world.
  4. Part IV — When Trust Breaks Down. A world competing on truth, manufactured doubt, the shroud of misinformation, and the crisis in the universities.
  5. Part V — The Path Forward. Voting on science, building an education and a career, who pays when the grid blinks, and what happens when fluency is mistaken for thinking.

The four tools

The book ends by handing you four questions. The situation comes first — you pick the tool from where you are actually standing. Each is demonstrated in its home chapter before it is named, and the names matter mainly because a name is what lets you hand a question to somebody else without the book in the room.

  • The hypothesis space (Ch. 5) — when you need a question worth asking, yours and not the textbook’s. What isn’t sitting right, and what if the anomaly isn’t a mistake? The territory of questions a field could still ask, visible only once you know where the known answers stop. Read the companion piece →
  • Claim, Evidence, Reasoning (Ch. 15) — when the evidence is within your reach. What is the claim, what is the evidence, does the reasoning connect them? Then the two that keep you honest: what would change my mind, and who benefits from my believing this? Read the companion piece →
  • The Welcome Test (Ch. 11) — when you can’t check the claim yourself, which is most claims that matter. Does the claim survive testing, and does the person making it welcome the test? You’re judging a claimant’s relationship to being checked, not re-running the science. Read the companion piece →
  • The Reaching Standard (Ch. 12) — when checking runs out before the decision does. Which of the real options in front of you is better? Rank them, act on the better one, and keep the ideal as the direction you’re moving in — reached for, never fully held. Read the companion piece →

Two tools for claims you can check. One for claimants you can’t. One for acting anyway. Not certainty. Not expertise. A practice.

All four are on one sheet — Teaching #13, “The Four Tools — the Method at a Glance” — built to be handed to someone without the book in the room.

The other named ideas

Nine ideas in all are carried by a breakout box, six of those also by a figure — two of them the Welcome Test and the Reaching Standard above. They are a large part of why this site exists: the book states them, and the site puts them to work on new material.

  • Pasteur’s Quadrant (Ch. 7). Why the fight between basic and applied research is a false one, and what fills the corner that answers both questions at once.
  • The chain and the rope (Ch. 9). A chain of inference is only as strong as its weakest link. A rope is stronger than any single strand in it — which is why converging lines of independent evidence, and not any one study, are what actually carry a scientific claim. Read the companion piece →
  • The four conditions for changing a mind (Ch. 12). What has to be true before anyone revises a belief — including you. Read the companion piece →
  • Two models for funding science (Ch. 13). Curiosity-driven and mission-driven research are usually argued about as rivals. The best work needs both, and the mistake is funding one corner on purpose and assuming the other shows up for free. Read the companion piece →
  • What share of the federal budget actually goes to science. (Ch. 14). Most people guess high, by a lot. The real number changes what the argument is about. Read the companion piece →
  • The instrument versus the reasoner (Ch. 16). One kind of machine tells you how sure it is, region by region; the other gives every claim the same finish. The point isn’t to avoid AI — it’s to know which kind you’re holding. Read the companion piece →

A few more run through the book without a box of their own, and turn up often on this site: the master fisherman problem (mastery that never resolves into a formula you can hand somebody else), serendipity in Merton’s sense, the misinformation spectrum from scarcity to fabrication and the evidentiary self-defense that answers it, checking the checkers, and the shroud — the layer that settles over any good process the moment we stop testing it and start trusting it out of habit.

What this site is for

A book is finished when it is finished. The world it describes is not, and a printed page is frozen on the day it prints. This site is where the ideas above get used on new material, where the classroom work behind the book lives, and where the sources are kept in the open. The book points here by name and number throughout, and the numbers are stable, so a citation in the book keeps working.

  • The full bibliography — every source behind the book, by chapter. Nothing in the book was invented; this is where you check that.
  • The index of terms — every scientific, philosophical and educational term the book runs on, with a one-sentence definition, the page it appears on, and the articles that use it. Generated from the book itself, so the page numbers stay true.
  • Look Around You — reflection questions and companion reading for every chapter, written to be handed to a student directly.
  • The modular index — which piece fits whatever unit you are already teaching.
  • Evidence — the ideas above, applied.

A note on how this book was made

It started with seven years of my own classroom notes and journaling, handed to Claude to find the shape in. I used Claude, an AI system built by Anthropic, as a developmental-editing collaborator throughout the writing and revision of the book, and this site is built and kept current the same way. Every personal story, every claim about my own life, and every final editorial call is mine — and saying so out loud is the transparent use Chapters 15 and 16 argue teachers should be modeling for students rather than hiding from them.

A note on being wrong

A book arguing that science earns trust by being correctable would be a strange book to write as though its author had never been corrected. So the book says how sure it is as it goes, and says plainly where it got something wrong.

Here is one. An early draft of Chapter 4 claimed that scientists have a term for what happens when a research design throws off something better than the result you set out for — that they call it collateral knowledge. Then I went looking for the source, the way the book keeps insisting you should, and there wasn’t one: the phrase lives in financial law, not in laboratories. I had assembled a plausible-sounding term of art out of two words I liked and my own certainty — exactly the failure mode I spend whole chapters warning students about.

The real term is older and better. Serendipity — not in the lucky-break sense, but in the one Robert Merton spent fifty years defending: the trained capacity to notice that an unanticipated result is telling you something, and to go after it instead of filing it. The wreckage stayed in the book, because it is the most useful thing in that section.
