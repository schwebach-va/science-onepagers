Look Into This With Me: Growing a Student’s Question From Freshman Year to Dual Enrollment

Companion to Chapter 12 of Everybody’s Science

I trust a scientific community’s process over my own checks, in the lab or with a student. It’s kind of an always thing. Unfortunately, science doesn’t always answer the questions I need to know answers to. And so there’s a space between the process, what has been worked on, and what can be asked.

The questions students bring

My students have tons of neuroscience questions. They’re at an age where, as they go to college, they’re learning about how they learn. They’re learning about motivation and how relationships affect them, and they think a lot about how others are impacting how they feel.

A few students actually get into that space where they challenge me with scientific questions. When they do, it may be something like whether we can cure cancer. That is the holy grail of so much biomedical science. Every cancer is different, so responding to it requires me to be a lot more aware of the exact research on a particular cancer to know what the opportunity would be to even have a conversation.

What I say in the moment

I tend to reach for this: there is a lot of hope, and that’s why we are conducting research. Then I ask, “Would you like to look into this with me?” I am happy to read a paper you find that you think is interesting, and I can look for something too. Go ahead and send me an email, and we’ll talk a little more about your question.

Hopefully the student, if an older student, has actually sent something they found that they’re interested in. I read that paper and think about the context for how they’ve written their email, and I ask another question back to them. Sometimes I use AI to explore some opportunities in the area. Sometimes I use Google Scholar. I definitely read one or two articles that I think connect deeply to the question and interests of the student. Then I send that email back with a couple of questions and potential opportunities for the student to consider.

Dual enrollment: a paper on a question they own

They actually do quite a bit in my dual enrollment Biology 101 course, where every student writes a paper on their My Biology topic. This is a paper I help them with. I allow them to use AI to focus their question. I constantly give input in Flex about their topic, their motivation to study it, and their progress on the paper. With the milestones in the course, I engage their thinking process.

The assignment itself, milestone by milestone, is in Teaching #20, “Writing a Science Paper in DE Biology”, and students start it with the Paper Idea Form.

A lot of the work I have on this site is about supporting this kind of evidence-based thinking in a developmental way, so that a student can find a question and further it. Student-Authored Modules (Teaching #15, and #19) are part of that work.

Freshmen: the question grows out of an experience

For freshmen, it’s much harder to do. I get a couple that will pull a paper and have this conversation, but most students then need a level of engagement that is different.

That gets to the Blue Ridge Salamander project I’ve developed for them, where there’s more of a quest, more of an expedition, more of an involvement. The content is a little more handed to them, and the questions come from that experience. The question asking is contextual, about a biological or scientific principle.
