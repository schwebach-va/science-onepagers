Medicine, Dentistry, Nursing, PA, Optometry, Physical Therapy: The Exams and the Biology They Test

The short version

  • Almost every health career has its own entrance exam, and almost all of them start from the same introductory biology you are taking now: cells, membranes, proteins and enzymes, energy, genes and signals.
  • The tools on this site build that biology. The inventory below lists each one, the exam topics it covers, and a utility score out of 12.
  • Use them the way the research says people learn: predict before you look, explain it in your own words, draw it from memory, and come back to it more than once.
  • This is not a prep course. It is a map from what we do in class to where it shows up again.

Some of you want to be physicians. Others are headed for dentistry, nursing, optometry, physical therapy, pharmacy or work as a physician assistant. Each road has its own test, and the names change, but the biology underneath them is the same course you are in right now.

That matters because students often learn a topic for this semester’s exam and lose the context for why it was taught. Signal transduction, the electron transport chain and protein trafficking will come back on the MCAT, the DAT, the PA-CAT and the TEAS, and later on licensing exams. I want you to know that now, and to have one page you can come back to when you start preparing.

After school come licensing exams: the USMLE or COMLEX for physicians, the NCLEX for nurses, and board exams for dentists, PAs and therapists. They test the same foundations applied to patients. A later update of this page will map them too.

How to use these tools if you are headed for one of these exams

  1. Predict before you press. Every step in the animations asks you a question first. Answer it, and commit. Being wrong with confidence is how the right answer sticks.
  2. Explain it out loud, or on paper, without looking. One sentence for the whole process. If you cannot say it, you do not have it yet.
  3. Draw it from memory. Sketch the pathway, label it, then check it against the figure. These exams reward people who can rebuild a mechanism, not people who recognize one.
  4. Come back to it. Once this semester, again before the final, and again the year you prepare for your exam. Spaced practice beats one long session.
  5. Read it as an experiment. The MCAT and DAT ask what happens when something is blocked, missing or changed. Some tools, like The Protein Route, let you block a step and watch what piles up. Do that on purpose.
  6. Use the practice questions. The Checkpoint Companion and the self-checks answer every question with the reasoning, and with a link to where to study.

How the utility score works

Each tool gets 0 to 3 points on four questions, for a total out of 12.

QuestionWhat earns the points
C · Coverage3 when an exam outline names the topic itself; 2 when it sits inside a named category; 1 when it is background
D · Depth1 for vocabulary; 2 for the mechanism; 3 for reasoning about an experiment or data, the way exam passages are written
R · Retrieval0 for watching; 1 for a check at the end; 2 for a prediction before each step; 3 for answering first and getting the reasoning back
U · Reuse1 for a single page; 2 when it remembers your progress or links to each question; 3 when you can open any step directly, with stills or video to come back to

These are first-pass scores from October 2026. I revise a tool’s score each time I rebuild it, and the rebuilds follow the research in Predict, Then Press, Sometimes.
