Living Wet Mounts Without an Aquarium: Moss, Ivy, Onion and Pond Water From Your Own Yard

The short version

  • A moss leaf does Elodea’s job. Most moss leaves are a single cell thick, so the chloroplasts are in plain view at 100× and 400×, and moss grows on almost every school campus in every season.
  • Pond water, a jar of dead leaves and a cut ivy stem cover the rest: algae, live protists, stomata and guard cells, and the leaves for a floating leaf disk lab.
  • Keep jars, not an aquarium. A clear jar on a windowsill needs no filter, no heater and almost no care.
  • Say no to turtle and reptile tank water. Those animals carry Salmonella, and their tank water and plants carry it too.
  • Never release a store-bought water plant. “Anacharis” is usually Egeria densa, an invasive species in Virginia.

Elodea is in every lab manual because its leaves are two cells thick and its chloroplasts stream around the cell. It is also hard to keep. Pet stores carry it some months and not others, it melts in a classroom tank over a long weekend, and the plant sold as “anacharis” is usually a different species that should never reach a stream. Every year the same scramble happens the week of the microscope lab.

The fix is to stop depending on one plant. What a microscope lab needs from a living specimen is a sheet of cells thin enough to see through, with something green inside. A yard, a pond edge and a kitchen supply that for free, and some of it is better than Elodea.

Pairings that teach something

One slide shows a cell. Two slides side by side make an argument. These pairs each answer a question students can state before they look.

The questionPair theseWhat settles it
What makes a plant cell different from an animal cell?Moss leaf beside prepared human epitheliumThe wall and the chloroplasts in one, neither in the other, at the same magnification
Why stain anything?Onion in water beside onion in iodineThe nucleus that was there all along, made visible, with the stain as the only change
Do all plant cells photosynthesize?Moss leaf beside onion skin, then the ivy peelGreen leaf cells, colorless bulb cells, and guard cells as the green exception in the leaf’s skin
How do gases get into a leaf?Ivy peel, then the floating leaf disk lab with ivy leavesStomata on the slide; disks that sink when the air is pulled out and float as oxygen collects
Is a dead, stained cell the same as a living one?Prepared Amoeba slide beside a live pond or infusion dropMovement, feeding and contractile vacuoles that a prepared slide cannot show
Prokaryote or eukaryote?A bacteria slide, a pond drop with a blue-green filament, and SpirogyraThe cyanobacterium makes food with no chloroplast and no nucleus; Spirogyra has both
How big is a cell, really?Moss leaf cells and onion cells, each measured against the field of viewTwo numbers in micrometers that differ by several times, from cells that look alike

Safety calls

SourceThe riskWhat to do
Turtle, frog or other reptile and amphibian tanksThese animals commonly carry Salmonella without looking sick, and it is in their water and on the plants in it.Do not use them. Not the water, not the plants. Use a fish tank or a pond instead.
Pond and puddle water, infusion jarsMixed bacteria, sometimes pathogens, especially near geese, livestock or runoffCollect from clean-looking water away from animal waste. Gloves for handling, no mouth pipetting, cover cuts, wash hands after. Keep jars lidded loosely and dispose of cultures by your district’s procedure.
Iodine (Lugol’s)Stains skin and clothes; irritating to eyesGoggles and gloves for the iodine step; dropper bottles, not open beakers.
English ivySap can irritate some people’s skin. English ivy is also on Virginia’s invasive list.Gloves when cutting and peeling; wash hands. Bag the trimmings for the trash rather than the compost pile.
Store-bought water plants“Anacharis” is usually Egeria densa, and hydrilla turns up as a hitchhiker. Both are on Virginia’s invasive plant list.Never put them in a pond, a ditch or a drain. Dry them out and bag them for the trash.
Collecting outdoorsRules on public land; allergies to mold and grassCollect at home or on school grounds, a pinch at a time. A student with mold or grass allergies works with the onion and prepared slides.
GlassCut fingers from slides and cover slipsA glass box at every bench; broken glass is never picked up by hand.

Keeping material going without an aquarium

An aquarium is a pet. It needs a filter, a heater, water changes and someone on holidays. The four setups below need a windowsill and a minute a week, and each one fails gently: if it dies, the yard still has more.

  1. The moss box. A clear plastic food container with a lid, a layer of soil, and a few clumps of moss pressed onto it. Mist it when the lid stops fogging. Bright, indirect light. It stays green for months and gives a leaf whenever a lab needs one.
  2. The pond jar. A clear quart jar, two-thirds pond water, a spoonful of the bottom sediment, a few strands of green algae and one dead leaf. Lid on loosely. Bright light but not direct sun, which cooks a jar. Top it up with rainwater or tap water that has stood a day. The community inside changes week to week, which is half the interest.
  3. The leaf infusion. A handful of dead leaves or dry grass, rinsed, in a jar of pond water or standing tap water. Loose lid, indirect light, and leave it alone. It turns cloudy and smells within a few days as bacteria bloom; the protists that eat them follow in about a week and peak over the next two or three. Start it two weeks before the lab day it serves. A commercial version of the method uses timothy hay and spring water, and yard leaves work on the same principle.
  4. The ivy jar. Cut ivy stems standing in water on the windowsill. They stay fresh for weeks and often root, which makes them a steady supply for leaf peels and for leaf disks.

Who you are likely to meet in a drop of pond water

A first look at pond water is a crowd of moving shapes with no names. A handful of organisms turn up in most freshwater samples, and each one carries an idea from the course.

OrganismHow to recognize itThe idea it carries
ParameciumA clear slipper shape covered in cilia, swimming fast in a spiral and backing up when it bumps somethingOne cell doing everything: feeding, moving, getting rid of water with contractile vacuoles
VorticellaA bell on a thin stalk, anchored to debris; the stalk snaps into a coil and slowly stretches out againA contractile protein system inside a single cell
StentorA large trumpet, often blue-green, sweeping food in with a ring of ciliaHow big one cell can get, and why it carries a giant nucleus with many copies of its genome
EuglenaA green spindle with a red eyespot near the front, changing shape as it movesA cell that both photosynthesizes and eats, which troubles the plant–animal split
DiatomsGolden-brown boat or disk shapes with glassy, patterned walls, some gliding slowlyA cell wall made of silica; a large share of the oxygen made in water
Desmids such as ClosteriumBright green crescents or paired halves joined at a narrow waistSymmetry and chloroplasts in a single cell
SpirogyraLong unbranched green threads with a spiral chloroplast in each cellChloroplast shape, and a chain of identical cells
Cyanobacteria such as OscillatoriaBlue-green filaments that glide and sway slowly, with no visible chloroplast or nucleusA prokaryote that photosynthesizes; the ancestor of the chloroplast

Tips at the scope

  • Start low. Scan pond drops at 40× and 100×; a ciliate crosses the field at 400× before anyone can focus on it.
  • Slow the swimmers. A few cotton fibers pulled from a ball under the cover slip make a maze that traps fast ciliates. A drop of a methyl cellulose slowing solution does the same.
  • Turn the light down. Live, unstained cells are nearly transparent. Close the diaphragm until edges appear.
  • Take from the bottom. Most of the life in a jar sits in the sediment and on the glass, not in the clear middle.
  • Photograph it. A phone or an iPad held to the eyepiece turns a moving organism into evidence a student can label later.
