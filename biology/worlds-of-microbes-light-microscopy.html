An Introduction to the Worlds of Microbes and What We Can See Using Light Microscopy

Biology #23 Eight short sections, each about two minutes of reading · By J. R. Schwebach

I trained in infectious disease. I know how to look for the very small microbes I am studying, and I know what the dangerous ones do. What surprises me is how little I know about all the microbes out there: the range of things that are bigger, that are interacting with each other, that are important for evolution, and that are everywhere. A drop of pond water on a classroom slide makes the point fast. One year it holds tardigrades, a nematode big enough to spot on the scanning lens, and moving protists none of us can name on sight. The next, my pond water is nearly dead, and we wait on a hay infusion to come alive.

1 · How small, and how far a light microscope reaches

In the 1670s Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, a Dutch cloth merchant, ground tiny glass beads into lenses, looked at rainwater and scrapings from his own teeth, and drew what he saw: “animalcules” that swam, spun and multiplied. Drawing was the record then, and it is still how you learn to see a cell, because a drawing forces you to decide what is actually there.

Your classroom microscope reaches much farther than his, but it has a hard floor. Light cannot separate two points closer than about 0.2 µm, even with an oil-immersion lens, and about half a micrometre with your 40× objective. A bacterium 1 to 2 µm long shows up as a dot or a short rod. You can see its shape and how it moves, never its insides. A ribosome, at 25 nm, is far below the floor.

2 · Three domains, and the one you cannot see

Every living thing falls into one of three domains. Bacteria and Archaea are prokaryotes: no nucleus, no membrane-bound organelles. Eukarya have a nucleus and organelles, and the domain includes every protist, fungus, plant and animal. So the first question at the microscope is always the same: is there a nucleus?

Archaea are the domain no light microscope will ever show you. They look like bacteria. Carl Woese found them in the 1970s by comparing the genes for ribosomal RNA, not by looking. They are in the mud at the bottom of your pond, making methane, and in soil and seawater, where many of them turn ammonia into nitrite, a key step in the nitrogen cycle. No archaeon is known to cause disease in people. They are underappreciated and seriously important for all of life on Earth.

Bacteria are just as varied. Thermus aquaticus, found in the hot springs of Yellowstone, makes enzymes that work best at about 70 °C, temperatures that would wreck most of yours. Its DNA polymerase, Taq, is the enzyme that makes PCR work.

3 · Green, but not algae: the cyanobacteria

The green chains on your cyanobacteria slide are bacteria. Anabaena grows as beads on a string, with a few larger, paler cells called heterocysts that pull nitrogen gas out of the air. Oscillatoria is a smooth filament that glides and slowly sways, which is how it got its name. Neither has a nucleus or a chloroplast; the whole cell does photosynthesis on folded internal membranes.

Cyanobacteria invented the kind of photosynthesis that splits water and releases oxygen. About 2.4 billion years ago they changed the air of the whole planet. In warm, nutrient-rich water some of them, like Microcystis, bloom into green scums that can make toxins, which is why Virginia lakes post warnings in late summer.

4 · Protists: easy to see, hard to understand

Protists are large and easier to see, but much harder to understand. The word is a drawer, not a branch: a protist is any eukaryote that is not a plant, an animal or a fungus. On the Tree of Life the protists sit on branches all across Eukarya, and some are closer to us than to each other. Amoeba proteus is more closely related to you than it is to Paramecium.

Paramecium swims with thousands of beating cilia and carries two kinds of nucleus. Pond water is hypotonic, so water leaks into it all day; a contractile vacuole swells and snaps shut to pump it back out. Amoeba crawls on false feet and engulfs its food. Euglena has chloroplasts and an eyespot and swims toward light, then feeds on whatever it can absorb in the dark. Is it a plant or an animal? It is neither, and the question is the lesson.

The green algae on your slides are protists too, though their relatives are not. Volvox is a hollow ball of hundreds to thousands of swimming cells. Spirogyra, with its spiral chloroplast, belongs to the freshwater algae that are the closest living relatives of land plants. Diatoms build shells of glass and make about a fifth of the oxygen on Earth. And not everything with spores on a slide is a protist: wheat rust is a fungus, in the same kingdom as mushrooms, and fungi are closer relatives of animals than of plants.

5 · Not every wriggler is a microbe

A drop of pond water also holds animals: many cells, organized into tissues, with a mouth and a gut. A nematode is a roundworm that thrashes in an S-shape and is often big enough to see on the scanning lens. A rotifer spins a crown of cilia at its head end to pull food in. A tardigrade, the water bear, walks on eight stubby legs and can survive drying out, freezing and vacuum. Johann Goeze drew the first one in 1773 and called it a little water bear. Hydra, a tiny relative of jellyfish, sits on a plant stem and stings its prey with its tentacles; green hydras carry algae inside their own cells.

6 · We came from prokaryotes

We evolved from prokaryotic cells. The best evidence says a eukaryotic cell began as a partnership. An archaeon, related to the Asgard archaea found in deep-sea mud, took in a bacterium related to today’s Alphaproteobacteria. The bacterium stayed, kept dividing inside its host, and became the mitochondrion. Mitochondria still carry their own small circular DNA and bacterial-type ribosomes, and still divide by splitting in two. Later, a eukaryote took in a cyanobacterium, and that became the first chloroplast.

So when you follow cellular respiration in the next unit, the ATP-making machinery you study was once a free-living bacterium, and the oxygen waiting at the end of the chain was put into the air by cyanobacteria.

7 · Could a cut from pond water make you sick?

On the first Lab 4 day this year a cover slip broke and stabbed me in the finger, and my first worry was the dirty water: could I get Giardia this way? The answer turns on how each microbe gets in. Giardia has to be swallowed. Its tough cysts pass through the stomach and settle in the small intestine, so a cut on the hand will not give you giardiasis; drinking untreated pond water could.

A cut is a different door, and a few pond microbes use it. Freshwater bacteria such as Aeromonas can infect a wound, even in water that looks clean. The amoeba Acanthamoeba can enter through a cut, but serious infections are rare and happen mostly in people with weakened immune systems. Naegleria, the warm-water amoeba, causes harm only when water goes up the nose. Wash a cut right away with soap and running water, cover it, and tell your teacher; see a doctor if redness, swelling or warmth spreads. Gloves and handwashing in lab are mostly about keeping anything from reaching your mouth.

8 · What a drop of water tells us

Infectious disease is one side of the microbial world, and it is a small one. On the other side are the amoebas, the ciliates, the algae and the archaea that live their lives with no interest in us at all. When a student puts wheat rust next to Paramecium next to Anabaena and asks which domain each one belongs to, the variety in their cell structures tells us so much about evolution and the forces of evolution. Some of these things are infectious. There are so many more archaea that have never infected a human, are underappreciated, and are seriously important for all of life on Earth.

The best way to learn them is to keep looking: a jar of pond water, a hay infusion on a windowsill, moss from your own yard. Each new drop is a chance to ask the same three questions. Is there a nucleus? Is it one cell or many? Where does it sit on the tree of life?
