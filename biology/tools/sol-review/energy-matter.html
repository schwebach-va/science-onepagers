Sun to sugar to ATP, in five ideas

Idea 1BIO.2e · LT 3.1Battlefield review

ATP releases energy by losing its last phosphate

ATP carries three phosphate groups in a row. When a cell needs energy right now, it snaps off the last phosphate, and the energy in that bond goes to work: a muscle contracts, a pump moves an ion. What remains is ADP, a battery with one charge spent. Energy from glucose puts the phosphate back on, so a cell rebuilds the same ATP thousands of times instead of using it up.

On the test
The trap is the recharge. Adding a phosphate to ADP stores energy; removing one from ATP releases it.
Draw it
Draw ATP as a string of three beads. Snip the last bead and draw a spark labeled “energy for work.” Label what is left “ADP,” then draw an arrow back to ATP labeled “energy from glucose.”

Idea 2BIO.2e · LT 3.2Battlefield review

A leaf builds sugar from carbon dioxide and water, powered by light

A plant builds sugar out of two plain molecules: carbon dioxide pulled from the air through its leaves and water drawn up from the soil. Out come glucose, which holds the energy, and oxygen, which leaves through the same leaf openings. Light is not one of the ingredients. It is the power source, and its energy ends up stored in the bonds of the sugar. The molecules you breathe out are the ones a plant takes in.

On the test
A diagram will show arrows going into and out of a leaf or a chloroplast. Sort by direction: what the plant brings in is a reactant, what it makes or lets go is a product, and light is the energy that drives the change.
Draw it
Draw a leaf. Add arrows: carbon dioxide in from the air, water up from the roots, sunlight striking the top, glucose stored inside, oxygen out. Circle the sunlight and write “energy, not an ingredient.”

Idea 3BIO.2e · BIO.1 · LT 3.2Battlefield reviewSchwebach Recommendation

Photosynthesis runs as fast as its scarcest supply allows

More light speeds photosynthesis up, and so does more carbon dioxide, until some other supply runs short and the rate levels off. Water matters too: a wilting plant closes its leaf openings and stops taking in carbon dioxide. Temperature works through the enzymes inside the chloroplast, so a little warmth helps and too much heat slows everything down. On the SOL this idea usually arrives as a graph, so practice reading where a rate curve rises and where it flattens.

On the test
Rate questions come as a table or a graph. Where the numbers stop climbing, the factor being changed is no longer the limit; something else has run short.
Draw it
Sketch a graph with “light” along the bottom and “oxygen bubbles per minute” up the side. Draw a line that rises, then goes flat. Label the flat part “something else is running short.”
Check it

Idea 4BIO.2e · LT 3.3Battlefield review

Oxygen decides how much ATP a cell gets back

Every cell starts breaking down glucose the same way, in the cytoplasm. If oxygen is there, the mitochondria finish the job and the cell gets back about 36 ATP from one glucose. If oxygen runs out, the cell stops early and ferments, which keeps the first step going for only 2 ATP. Your muscles in a hard sprint make lactic acid this way; yeast makes alcohol and carbon dioxide, which is why bread rises. A cell living on 2 ATP per glucose burns through sugar far faster to keep up.

On the test
Look for the word oxygen in the question. With oxygen: mitochondria and about 36 ATP. Without it: fermentation in the cytoplasm, 2 ATP, and sugar used up much faster.
Draw it
Draw one glucose at a fork in the road. One path: “oxygen → mitochondrion → about 36 ATP.” The other: “no oxygen → fermentation → 2 ATP,” splitting into “muscle: lactic acid” and “yeast: alcohol + carbon dioxide.”

Idea 5BIO.2e · BIO.8 · LT 3.4Battlefield review

Carbon goes around the loop; energy passes through once

Carbon moves in a loop, and living things run both halves. Photosynthesis pulls carbon dioxide out of the air and locks its carbon into sugar. Respiration breaks that sugar down and releases the carbon as carbon dioxide again, in every organism that respires: animals, plants, fungi and bacteria. Decomposers return carbon too, and they do it the same way, by respiring as they digest dead matter. Matter cycles around this loop; energy passes through once and leaves as heat.

On the test
When a question asks how living things put carbon back into the air, the answer is respiration, decomposers included. The products of one process are the raw materials of the other.
Draw it
Draw a chloroplast and a mitochondrion side by side. Arrows from the mitochondrion to the chloroplast carry carbon dioxide and water; arrows back carry glucose and oxygen. Draw the sun’s arrow coming in at the top and a heat arrow leaving at the bottom.
Check it
6 practice questions on this idea

Four checks for any energy question

  1. Which way is the energy moving? Into sugar is photosynthesis; out of sugar into ATP is respiration.
  2. Is there oxygen? Find the word in the question. It decides between about 36 ATP and 2.
  3. Count what goes in and what comes out. The two equations hold the same six carbons, twelve hydrogens and eighteen oxygens, only in opposite directions.
  4. Find the flat part of the graph. Where a rate stops rising, another factor has become the limit.

Try the checks on the deer question: sunlight reaches clover, the deer eats the clover, the deer runs. Energy moves into sugar in the clover, then out of sugar into ATP in the deer’s muscle, so the answer runs sunlight, sugar in clover, sugar in deer, ATP in muscle. Try that one.

Three Schwebach Recommendations

These three ideas sit at the edge of most reviews, and each one costs students points on the state test.

Recommendation 1Schwebach Recommendation

Some cells photosynthesize with no chloroplasts at all

Why it is here: Gap between the course and the state guide: cyanobacteria are prokaryotes, and Virginia students struggle to classify single-celled organisms that photosynthesize (about a third answered that question correctly on the form released in 2026).

Photosynthesis needs chlorophyll to capture light, not the chloroplast itself. Photosynthetic prokaryotes such as cyanobacteria hold chlorophyll in membranes in their cytoplasm. Chloroplasts likely began as cells like these.

Recommendation 2Schwebach Recommendation

Plants respire too, in every living cell, day and night

Why it is here: A common misconception: students learn photosynthesis for plants and respiration for animals. Plants run both, and a root or a seed in the dark runs on respiration alone.

Leaves make sugar and send it through the plant. Root cells, like all plant cells, use cellular respiration in their mitochondria to break that sugar down and make ATP. Plants respire, day and night, in every living cell.

Recommendation 3Schwebach Recommendation

Without oxygen, a cell burns through sugar much faster

Why it is here: The hardest cell-energy question on the form Virginia released in 2026 asked what an organism does with sugar when there is no oxygen. Fewer than half of Virginia students answered it correctly.

A cell that gets 2 ATP from each glucose instead of about 36 has to break down roughly eighteen times as much sugar for the same energy. That is why a sealed bag of yeast and sugar runs through its sugar quickly, and why a sprinting muscle that has run short of oxygen tires fast.
