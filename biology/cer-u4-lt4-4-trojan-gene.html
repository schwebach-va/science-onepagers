The Trojan Gene: When the Biggest Fish Wins and the Population Loses

Biology #22 SOL BIO.5e, BIO.1 · one sheet, front and back

Fish farms want fish that reach market size sooner. One way to get them is genetic engineering: scientists add a growth-hormone gene to a fish egg, and the fish that hatches grows faster than its parents ever did. Researchers have engineered tilapia, a common farm fish, this way. The fish is now a genetically modified organism, a GMO, and every one of its cells carries the added gene.

On a farm, a bigger fish is the whole point. In a river, a bigger fish could be a problem, and the reason has to do with who gets to mate.

Two measurements that pull in opposite directions

Biologists at Purdue University tested the idea with the medaka, a small fish that mates every day, so many generations can be watched quickly. They gave medaka a growth-hormone gene and measured two things.

The first was survival. Fewer of the genetically modified young made it through early life: for every 100 wild young that survived, only about 70 of the modified young did.

The second was mating. Female medaka prefer large males. In wild fish, the researchers had already found that large males got about four times as many matings as small ones. In a later experiment they put one modified male, one wild male and one female in a tank. The modified males were 83 percent heavier, and they won about three of every four matings.

A gift that ruins the city

The researchers fed both numbers into a computer model of a wild population that a few modified fish had joined. Because modified males win most of the matings, the gene spreads, and more of each new generation carries it. Because those young die more often, each generation is smaller than the last. In the model the gene took over and the whole population shrank toward zero within a few dozen generations, about 40 in the first model and about 50 in the second. The team named this the Trojan gene effect, after the wooden horse the city of Troy pulled inside its own walls.

The evidence has limits. It comes from lab tanks and a model, not a real river, and a later study of modified carp found no mating advantage at all. That is why regulators treat each modified animal on its own terms. The first engineered fish approved for food in the United States, a fast-growing salmon approved in 2015, may be raised only as sterile females in tanks on land.

Genetic engineering
Changing an organism's DNA on purpose, for example by adding a gene from another species.
GMO
A genetically modified organism: one whose DNA has been changed by genetic engineering.
Growth hormone
A protein signal that tells the body to grow; its gene is the instruction for making it.
Population
All the members of one species living in one place and breeding with each other.
Generation
One step from parents to their offspring.
Model
A set of rules, often run on a computer, that predicts what will happen from measured numbers.

Practice: Claim, Evidence, Reasoning

Claim: one sentence that answers the question and could be shown wrong. Evidence: a value from Table 1, or a line from the reading. Reasoning: the principle that ties them together.

  1. Quick check · choose one

    In the medaka experiments, what did the growth-hormone gene do to the young fish?  (a) It doubled their survival. (b) It lowered early survival to about 70 for every 100 wild young. (c) It had no effect on survival. (d) It turned every young fish male.

  2. Quick check · choose one

    A gene that hurts survival still spreads through the population in the model. Why?  (a) Modified males win most matings, so more young carry the gene. (b) The gene moves from fish to fish through the water. (c) Wild fish are killed by the hormone. (d) Modified females lay more eggs.

  3. Quick check · choose one

    Which is the best reason to be careful with the Trojan gene claim?  (a) Medaka are too small to study. (b) The researchers did not measure anything. (c) Fish do not choose mates. (d) The results come from lab tanks and a computer model, not a real river.
