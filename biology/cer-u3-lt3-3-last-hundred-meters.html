The Last Hundred Meters: Why a 400-Meter Runner Slows Down

Biology #21 SOL BIO.2e, BIO.1 · one sheet, front and back

Watch the last hundred meters of a 400-meter race. The runners have been sprinting for less than a minute, and their strides are getting shorter. Nobody has run out of food. Their legs hold plenty of sugar. Something else is running short: the speed at which their cells can make ATP.

Oxygen gives the most ATP, but it arrives slowly

A muscle cell that has oxygen breaks glucose down completely, starting in the cytoplasm and finishing in the mitochondria. One glucose yields about 36 ATP. The catch is delivery. Oxygen has to travel from the lungs through the blood, and during a sprint the legs spend ATP faster than that supply can rebuild it.

Fermentation is fast and wasteful

When oxygen runs short, the cell keeps going by fermentation. It breaks glucose down only part of the way, in the cytoplasm, and gets 2 ATP. The rest of the energy stays locked in lactic acid. A sprinter's legs also hold a small store of ready phosphate that lasts a few seconds. Together these let a runner spend energy much faster than oxygen alone could pay for.

Researchers measured this in 20 highly trained Australian runners, each racing at the distance he or she trained for. Table 1 shows how much of each race's energy came from pathways that use oxygen. The rest came from pathways that do not.

The burn has more than one cause

For years people blamed lactic acid for the burn and for next-day soreness. Scientists now tell a longer story. Lactic acid splits apart in the cell into lactate and acid, and the acid and other byproducts build up as the race goes on. Researchers still debate how much each one tires a muscle. The lactate is not garbage, either: the heart and other muscles take it up and use it as fuel, and next-day soreness has other causes.

Yeast makes the same trade. In a sealed jar of sugar water, each glucose gives yeast only 2 ATP, so it must go through sugar far faster than yeast with air does.
