Wet Soil, Thirsty Roots: Why Road Salt Pulls Water Out of a Plant

Biology #17 SOL BIO.3, BIO.1 · one sheet, front and back

On a January night before a storm, trucks spread salt and brine across Virginia’s roads so the ice cannot bond to the pavement. The salt does its job, and then it goes somewhere. Plows throw salty slush onto the shoulder, passing cars lift a fine salt spray into the shrubs, and snowmelt carries dissolved salt into the soil along the road. By spring, a hedge beside a busy road can show leaves brown at the edges and tips, while the same kind of shrub fifty feet away looks fine. The odd part is that the soil around the brown hedge may be wet the whole time.

Which way the water goes

A root takes in water by osmosis. Water crosses the cell membrane from the side where it is more concentrated to the side where it is less concentrated, which means water moves toward the side with more dissolved particles. Normally the inside of a root cell holds more dissolved sugars and salts than the soil water, so water moves into the root. Virginia Cooperative Extension describes what happens when salt builds up in the soil: the direction reverses, and water moves out of the roots even when the soil is wet. The soil water has become hypertonic to the root cells.

Nothing in the cell pushes that water out. Osmosis is passive transport. It runs down the concentration gradient and costs the cell no ATP, just as water slipped out of the red onion cells under your microscope. In a plant cell, the stiff cell wall keeps its shape while the membrane and the cytoplasm shrink away from it. That pulling away is plasmolysis. A cell in that state cannot do its job, and leaves far from the root dry out at their edges first.

Turning the arrow around

Water in a cell that sits in fresh water pushes outward against the wall. That push, turgor pressure, is what keeps a watered plant standing and a crisp stalk of celery crisp. Some plants that live in salty places spend energy to protect it: protein pumps in their membranes use ATP to move salt out of the cytoplasm or lock it away in the vacuole, against its concentration gradient. That is active transport. A roadside boxwood does not do this well, so it loses the water instead.

Osmosis
The movement of water across a selectively permeable membrane, toward the side with more dissolved particles.
Hypertonic
Describes a solution with more dissolved particles than the cell. Water leaves the cell.
Hypotonic
Describes a solution with fewer dissolved particles than the cell. Water enters the cell.
Plasmolysis
The membrane and cytoplasm of a plant cell shrinking away from the cell wall as water leaves.
Turgor pressure
The push of water inside a plant cell against its cell wall. It keeps plants firm.
Passive transport
Movement across a membrane, from high concentration to low, that costs the cell no energy.
