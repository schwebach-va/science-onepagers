From a PCR Band to a Family Tree of Our Foods

A lab extension in two parts · Part 1 about 35 minutes, Part 2 about 55 minutes · A school iPad and Safari, no account · By J. R. Schwebach

Your gel gave you bands. The plant-check lane showed a band about 455 bases long for nearly every food, and for some foods the GMO-check lane showed a band about 200 bases long. A band tells you how long a piece of DNA is. It does not tell you what the piece says or where it came from. Scientists answer those questions with free public databases, and you will use the same ones. In Part 1 you look up what each band is made of. In Part 2 you line up one plant gene from ten organisms, eight of them foods, and build a family tree from the differences.

How PCR picked out those two pieces

PCR is DNA replication in a tube, the same copying you learned with DNA polymerase. Your kit's thermocycler ran each tube through three temperatures, 40 times over:

  1. 94 °C for 1 minute, denature. Heat breaks the hydrogen bonds between the bases, and the two strands come apart. Each strand can now act as a template.
  2. 59 °C for 1 minute, anneal. Two primers, short single strands of DNA about 20 bases long, base-pair with the template at the two edges of the target. The primers are what make PCR choose one stretch out of a whole genome.
  3. 72 °C for 2 minutes, extend. Taq polymerase, an enzyme from a bacterium that lives in hot springs, adds nucleotides to the 3′ end of each primer, matching A with T and C with G.

Part 1 · Look up what each band is made of

BLAST compares a sequence you give it against every sequence in the public database at the National Center for Biotechnology Information and lists the closest matches. Use these three sequences:

  • Band A stands in for your plant-check band. It is the first 455 bases of psbA, a chloroplast gene for part of Photosystem II, taken from corn. Your kit names its target only as “the Photosystem II gene,” so the stretch its primers copy may sit elsewhere in the same gene.
  • Band B stands in for your GMO-check band. It is the 195 bases between a pair of GMO-screening primers, read from the published genome sequence where those primers bind.
  • Band C is a bonus. It is the 118 bases between a second pair of GMO-screening primers. Some kits test for both targets.
  1. Open Nucleotide BLAST in a new Safari tab.
  2. Tap Copy under Band A, then paste into the box labelled Enter Query Sequence.
  3. Leave every other setting alone. Scroll down and tap the blue BLAST button. The search takes from 30 seconds to about two minutes.
  4. On the Descriptions list, read the top five rows. Record the scientific name, Per. Ident (percent of bases that match), Query Cover (how much of your sequence lined up) and the E value in Table 1.
  5. Go back, clear the box, and repeat with Band B. Try Band C if you have time.

Part 1 questions. Answer in your lab report.

  1. Look at the names in the top ten hits for Band A. What kinds of organisms are they? Use that list to explain why the plant check gives a band from nearly every plant food, cooked or raw.
  2. What organism did Band B come from? Is it a plant?
  3. Band B is part of a promoter, the stretch of DNA that tells RNA polymerase where to start reading a gene. Genetic engineers place this promoter in front of the gene they add to a crop. Using what you know about transcription, explain why borrowing a strong promoter makes sense.
  4. A food gave a band in the plant lane and nothing in the GMO lane. Your lab manual lists three reasons that could happen. Which reason does Band B help you understand, and why?
  5. The E value estimates how many matches this good you would expect to find by chance alone in a database this size. What does an E value near 0 (for example 1e−100) tell you about the match?

Part 2 · Line up one gene from ten organisms and build the tree

The plant-check gene, psbA, carries the instructions for the D1 protein of Photosystem II, the part of the light reactions that splits water. Every plant and every cyanobacterium makes it, and it has to work or the cell cannot photosynthesize. Because the protein is so important, its gene changes slowly over time, so it can be lined up across organisms whose lineages split hundreds of millions of years ago or more. On paper or in Notes, sketch the family tree you expect for the ten organisms. Then write one sentence each: which two foods you expect to be most alike, and which organism you expect to differ most from corn.

Step C · Align them with Clustal Omega

  1. Open Clustal Omega at the European Bioinformatics Institute in a new Safari tab.
  2. Under Sequence type, choose DNA.
  3. Paste the ten sequences into the box and tap Submit. Leave the tab open; the job usually finishes in about a minute.
  4. On the alignment, a * under a column means all ten organisms have the same base there. Scroll through the first 120 columns. Which organism most often breaks a run of stars?
  5. Find the Percent Identity Matrix under the results summary, and fill in Table 2.
  6. Open the Phylogenetic Tree view. Sketch the tree beside your prediction.
  7. Open the Tree of Life Explorer and find the grasses, the legumes, the mustards, the nightshades, the mosses and the cyanobacteria. Note where your tree agrees with it and where it does not.

Part 2 questions. Answer in your lab report.

  1. Which pair in Table 2 matched most closely? Did your tree place corn, rice, wheat and oat together on one branch?
  2. List corn’s percent identity with soybean, moss and the cyanobacterium from highest to lowest. What does that order suggest about when each lineage split from the line that led to corn?
  3. The cyanobacterium is a bacterium, yet its gene lines up with the chloroplast genes of plants along its whole length. Use what you know about where chloroplasts came from to explain why.
  4. Papaya and canola belong to the same plant order, Brassicales. Did your tree put them side by side? A gene that changes slowly makes an excellent universal plant check in Part 1. Explain why the same slow change makes it weaker at sorting close relatives.
  5. Write a CER for this claim: Corn is more closely related to rice than to soybean. Use at least two numbers from Table 2 and one feature of your tree as evidence.
