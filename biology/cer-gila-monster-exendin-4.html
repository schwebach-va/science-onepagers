The Gila Monster's Glycine: One Amino Acid That Keeps a Signal Alive

Biology #19 SOL BIO.2d, BIO.2c, BIO.5e · one sheet, front and back

The Gila monster lives in the deserts of Arizona and northern Mexico and spends most of its life underground. It can live on three or four big meals a year, raiding nests for eggs and young birds, and can swallow a third of its body weight at once. Its bite carries venom from glands in its lower jaw. In 1992, Dr. John Eng's lab at the VA Medical Center in the Bronx pulled a chain of 39 amino acids out of that venom and named it exendin-4.

The same lock on a beta cell

After you eat, cells lining your gut release a hormone called GLP-1, a chain of 30 or 31 amino acids. It travels through the blood to beta cells in the pancreas and binds a receptor on their surface. When blood glucose is high, that signal tells the cells to release more insulin. Exendin-4 matches GLP-1 at 16 of its first 30 positions. The sequence folds into a similar shape, and the shape fits the same receptor on human beta cells. The lizard makes its own GLP-1 from a separate gene; the exendin-4 gene is switched on only in its venom glands.

Glycine at position 2 jams the scissors

Your own GLP-1 lasts only a minute or two. The blood carries an enzyme called DPP-4 that works like scissors. Its active site grips the first two amino acids of a chain and snips them off, and it does this fast when the second amino acid is alanine. GLP-1 has alanine there. Once its first two amino acids are gone, GLP-1 no longer switches on the receptor. Exendin-4 has glycine in that spot, and DPP-4 barely cuts it. Exendin-4 stays whole until the kidneys filter it out of the blood, and its half-life stretches to about 2.4 hours. In the genetic code, the gap between those two amino acids can be a single base: the codon GCU codes for alanine and GGU codes for glycine.

Chemists copied it, then redesigned it

No one milks lizards for medicine. Chemists build an exact copy of exendin-4, called exenatide, and in 2005 the FDA approved it as Byetta for type 2 diabetes. A later team started from human GLP-1 and made two key changes. At position 2 they placed Aib, an amino acid that is not one of the 20 that ribosomes use, so no codon codes for it and no cell could put it there. They also attached a fatty acid that makes the molecule cling to albumin, a large blood protein too big for the kidneys to filter. The result, semaglutide, was approved as Ozempic in 2017. Its half-life is about one week. All three molecules fit the same receptor and carry the same message; they differ in how long the blood keeps them.
