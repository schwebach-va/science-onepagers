The Loop That Finishes the Job: Oxytocin and the Last Hours of Labor

Biology #24 DE Biology and Advanced Biology · flex CER · one sheet, front and back

In 1906 the British physiologist Henry Dale found that an extract of the posterior pituitary, a pea-sized gland under the brain, made the muscle of a uterus contract. The substance doing it was named oxytocin, from the Greek for “quick birth.” In 1953 Vincent du Vigneaud worked out what it is: a chain of nine amino acids, with two cysteines joined by a disulfide bond into a ring. Oxytocin became the first peptide hormone to have its amino acid sequence determined, and du Vigneaud then built it in the laboratory from scratch. That work earned him the 1955 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Made in the brain, released from the pituitary

Neurons in the hypothalamus make oxytocin and send it down their long axons to the posterior pituitary, which releases it into the blood. On the muscle cells of the uterus, oxytocin binds its receptor, a G protein-coupled receptor of 388 amino acids that passes through the membrane seven times. That is the same receptor family as the epinephrine receptor in Unit 5. Once the receptor is active, the muscle contracts more often, and with more force because its contractile machinery becomes more sensitive to calcium.

Stretch, signal, squeeze, repeat

During labor the baby’s head presses on the cervix, the opening at the bottom of the uterus. Stretch-sensing nerves there signal the hypothalamus, the posterior pituitary releases oxytocin, and the uterus contracts harder. A harder contraction presses the head down further, and that brings still more oxytocin. This is positive feedback: the response strengthens the very stimulus that caused it. The loop is called the Ferguson reflex, after the Canadian physiologist J. K. W. Ferguson. Oxytocin arrives in pulses that grow larger and more frequent as labor goes on, with a large pulse at the birth itself. Oxytocin also makes the lining of the uterus release prostaglandins, which help soften the cervix. The loop ends when the baby is born and the stretch is gone.

Most control loops in the body run the other way. In negative feedback, the product of a process turns down its own source and holds a level steady. Isoleucine shutting off threonine deaminase, the first enzyme of its own pathway, is one example. Positive feedback does not hold anything steady. It drives a process faster until something ends it.

Receptors set how hard the muscle answers

A hormone acts only on cells that carry its receptor. From 13–17 weeks of pregnancy to 37–41 weeks, oxytocin receptors in the uterine muscle rise about twelvefold. Table 1 shows receptor levels measured in uterine muscle from women in Sweden who were delivered by cesarean section, some before labor started and some during it. The loop also has a brake. Strong contractions and stress switch on the sympathetic nerves, and that lowers oxytocin release.

A hormone in an IV bag

Hospitals give synthetic oxytocin through an IV drip to start labor or to strengthen it. They give it again after delivery, to keep the uterus contracting so it separates the placenta and prevents heavy bleeding. In the Swedish study, women in advanced labor who had received an oxytocin infusion had fewer receptors, and the drop was larger with a higher dose and a longer infusion.

Hormone
A chemical messenger made in one place that travels in the blood to act elsewhere.
Receptor
A protein that binds one kind of signal molecule and changes what the cell does.
G protein-coupled receptor
A receptor that crosses the membrane seven times and passes its signal to a G protein inside the cell.
Positive feedback
A loop in which the response strengthens the stimulus, so the process speeds up until something ends it.
Negative feedback
A loop in which the response weakens the stimulus, so a level is held steady.
Posterior pituitary
The back lobe of the pituitary gland, which releases hormones made by neurons of the hypothalamus.
Prostaglandin
A small lipid signal made from fatty acids that acts on nearby tissue.
